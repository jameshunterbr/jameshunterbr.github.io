Fitting Distributions

In which I explore various software techniques in R and Python for fitting statistical distributions to data

I have been working for the past month on developing a synthetic dataset to support the clinical trial our laboratory is starting in our search for a cure for HIV. Clearly, this is an ambitious goal. Our previous clinical trials and experiments, as well as the work of many other labs around the world have given us confidence that our approach can bear fruit. What is a synthetic dataset? Why am I interested in such a thing?

A synthetic dataset is a set of hypothetical cases that mirror as closely as possible in silico data that we are likely to collect from the real participants in our clinical trial. Synthetic datasets are useful when the process of collecting data is slow (as in our case) or the sample size is very restricted (as in the case of many animal model experiments where both ethics and cost limit how many animals can be sacrificed for an experimental result). In our case, eventually, we will replace our analyses conducted on the synthetic dataset with the results from the participants themselves.

The experimental protocol of the clinical trial tells us what variables will be collected. The synthetic dataset will use the same variables. Obviously, one can’t simply make the data up although you see frequently in texts, blog posts or statistical package manuals examples of simple random data predicated on a set of normally or uniformly distributed data, such as in the following example.1

age <- runif(n = 100, min = 18, max = 65) # choose 100 random values between 18 and 65

The question I would like to address in this post is how we go about choosing values to use in the variables we create for our synthetic dataset. These values should mimic as closely as possible the real values we are likely to encounter in the clinical trial. In only very few cases do we lack precedents or models that we can use for reasonable values for these variables. There are prior studies or trials that have reported values for the variables. They may not have the same objective or deal with exactly the same population as the real sample for the trial will, but they need to be “close enough” to make the analyses based on them reasonable approximations for the real data.

Fitting a Distribution for a Numeric Variable

There are many stages to preparing a synthetic dataset. In this post, I will limit myself to how we choose the distribution to use to set the values for a numeric variable. For example, in our clinical trial, one of the variables we collect on our participants is their age in years. Our trial will be limited to adults between 18 and 65 years – a common range for clinical trials. We also choose I will need to choose ages for 70 individuals (our “n”) within that range.

Our Precedent for the Age Variable

From 2017 - 2020, our lab conducted a smaller trial (30 individuals) of a similar strategy for HIV cure.2 Among the demographic variables about the participants in that trial, we recorded the age in years at the beginning of the trial.

As with any clinical trial, we need to assure that the members of the cohort reflect the population we are trying to measure, people living with HIV (“PLHIV”) in Brazil. For age, that means comparing the profile of the ages of participants to other analyses of this group. However, I will leave that question open here and focus on the distribution of ages to use as a model for age in the synthetic cohort for the new trial.
